### <b id="Decision">Cognitive Warfare Targets Decision-Making, Not Just Belief</b>  
During the “Cognitive Warfare & The Battle for Perception” panel, David Pitts described cognitive warfare as the use of synthetic realities, deepfakes, and disinformation to influence perception and undermine decision autonomy. The objective is not simply to make audiences believe something false, but to erode trust in institutions and in the information itself. When trust declines, decision-making becomes uncertain. That uncertainty is the operational effect.  

Ellen McCarthy focused on where this occurs. She argued that the frontline of cognitive warfare is the moment between receiving information and making a decision. Decision-makers are now forced to act in environments saturated with low-quality, manipulated, or conflicting information. Adversaries do not need to convince someone of a specific narrative. Creating uncertainty is enough. If leaders hesitate because they cannot confidently determine what is true, the decision process is degraded.  

Shawn Chenoweth explained why this approach is attractive to adversaries. States that cannot compete militarily or economically can instead compete asymmetrically in the information environment. He emphasized that information only matters when it drives action. Manipulating how information is interpreted allows adversaries to influence behavior indirectly. Because the internet functions as an environment that institutions and individuals must participate in, shaping that environment provides broad access to decision-making processes.  

The panel also distinguished between multiple forms of manipulation. Disinformation is intentionally false information. Misinformation is the unintentional spread of that information. Chenoweth also identified missing information, where relevant facts are removed or unavailable, and rhetoric information, where claims are framed in technically debatable but misleading ways. Each contributes to uncertainty. The objective is not simply spreading falsehoods, but degrading confidence in the information environment itself.  

When decision-makers cannot confidently assess what is true, responses slow and coordination weakens. Cognitive warfare targets the decision environment itself, rather than any single belief.

### <b id="Ecosystem">The Information Ecosystem Must Be Treated as Critical Infrastructure</b>  
Building on this, Ellen McCarthy argued that the information ecosystem should be treated like foundational infrastructure, comparable to food or water. Institutions, governments, and the public now depend on the integrity of information systems to function. When the information environment is degraded by manipulation, uncertainty, or low-quality data, decision-making across those systems is degraded as well. Cognitive warfare therefore targets a national dependency.  

If uncertainty in the information environment can slow decisions or undermine trust, protecting information integrity becomes a national security requirement. The objective is preserving the reliability of the information ecosystem itself. Without that reliability, even accurate intelligence or correct assessments may not be acted on confidently.  

David Pitts extended this argument by emphasizing data integrity. He warned about the potential for malicious or manipulated data to be injected into datasets and analytic workflows. If adversaries contaminate the information pipeline, downstream analysis becomes unreliable. This includes corrupted sources, poisoned datasets, or manipulated contextual information. The risk is not just misleading content, but compromised decision-support systems.  

Countering cognitive warfare therefore begins with protecting the information ecosystem. That includes maintaining data integrity, evaluating sources, and preventing manipulation from entering analytic pipelines. Once the integrity of the information environment is protected, countermeasures can move to identifying and countering influence operations directly.  

Treating information as critical infrastructure reframes cognitive warfare. The threat is not only disinformation itself, but the erosion of trust and reliability across systems that support decision-making. Protecting those systems becomes the starting point for countering cognitive warfare.  

### <b id="Scales">AI Scales Cognitive Warfare and Expands the Attack Surface of the Information Ecosystem</b>  
Artificial intelligence increases the speed, scale, and precision of cognitive warfare. During the panel, David Pitts pointed to synthetic realities, deepfakes, and AI-generated content as tools that allow adversaries to shape perception at scale. These capabilities reduce the cost of producing convincing narratives and make it possible to rapidly generate large volumes of tailored information designed to influence specific audiences.  

Shawn Chenoweth emphasized that adversaries already compete asymmetrically in the information environment. AI strengthens this approach by making manipulation faster and more adaptive. Instead of static disinformation campaigns, adversaries can generate iterative messaging, refine narratives, and respond to events in real time. This compresses the timeline between an event and the narrative surrounding it, increasing the likelihood that manipulated interpretations shape early understanding.  

Ellen McCarthy also discussed the use of small, targeted language models designed for specific audiences. These models operate on constrained datasets and are built to address narrow cognitive warfare scenarios. Influence operations no longer need to be broad or generic. Messaging can be tailored to particular populations, communities, or decision-makers. This increases the precision of cognitive warfare and reduces the likelihood that manipulation is easily recognized as coordinated.  

AI also increases the volume of competing narratives decision-makers must process. Synthetic media, generated narratives, and targeted messaging amplify ambiguity in an already saturated environment. As the cost of generating content falls, adversaries can flood the information ecosystem with competing interpretations, reinforcing uncertainty at critical decision points.

### <b id="Poison">Data Poisoning and Deception as Infrastructure-Level Attacks</b>  
David Pitts raised the risk of data poisoning and emphasized the importance of data integrity. His concern was not limited to disinformation circulating publicly, but malicious data entering the datasets and information systems that analysts, institutions, and AI models rely on. If adversaries can inject manipulated information into those pipelines, downstream analysis becomes unreliable. The attack is no longer just against audiences, but against the information infrastructure itself.  

This shifts cognitive warfare from manipulating outputs to manipulating inputs. Instead of convincing people directly, adversaries can contaminate the data used to produce analysis. This can include poisoned open-source intelligence, manipulated contextual data, biased datasets, or fabricated signals designed to appear legitimate. When these inputs are incorporated into analytic workflows, the resulting conclusions may appear credible while being based on corrupted information. The deception occurs upstream, before a decision-maker even encounters the output.  

Shawn Chenoweth linked this to deception as a longstanding component of warfare. Deception has always been used to mislead adversaries, and AI increases both the speed and scale at which deception can be conducted. Data poisoning extends this concept into the information ecosystem. Rather than misleading a specific audience, adversaries can shape the data environment that informs decisions across institutions.  

This becomes more consequential as AI systems are integrated into analytic workflows. AI models rely on large datasets and pattern recognition. If those datasets are manipulated, the model’s outputs reflect the manipulation. This creates the possibility of persistent influence, where corrupted data produces repeated misleading outputs over time. The risk is not a single false narrative, but sustained distortion of analysis.  

Data poisoning therefore represents a direct attack on the integrity of the information ecosystem. If information is critical infrastructure, poisoned data functions as infrastructure sabotage. The objective is to degrade trust in analysis, introduce uncertainty into decision-making, and influence outcomes indirectly.


### <b id="Counter">Countering Cognitive Warfare Through Prebunking and Information Defense</b>  
Shawn Chenoweth emphasized that cognitive warfare can be countered preemptively. Rather than waiting to debunk false claims after they spread, he described the effectiveness of warning audiences before a disinformation campaign unfolds. He referenced a French election in which officials anticipated a GRU information operation and publicly identified it in advance. When the campaign materialized, its impact was reduced because audiences had already been primed to recognize the manipulation. This shifts defense from reactive debunking to proactive prebunking.  

Prebunking aligns with treating the information ecosystem as critical infrastructure. If cognitive warfare creates uncertainty at the decision point, reducing that uncertainty before manipulation spreads becomes a defensive measure. By identifying likely influence operations early, institutions can preserve trust and reduce the effectiveness of adversarial narratives. The goal is to strengthen how information is evaluated, not to dictate conclusions.  
 
Speakers also discussed targeted defensive tools. Small language models trained on constrained datasets can be designed to address specific cognitive warfare scenarios. These models are intended to provide context, clarification, and corrective information for narrowly defined audiences that may be targeted by influence operations. This represents a shift toward defensive cognitive warfare: strengthening segments of the information ecosystem before adversaries exploit them.  

These approaches move cognitive warfare defense upstream. Instead of reacting after narratives spread, prebunking and targeted defensive tools attempt to harden the information environment and reduce the opportunity for adversaries to create uncertainty at critical decision points.

### <b id="Propaganda">The Risk of Counter-Cognitive Warfare Becoming Propaganda</b>  
These defensive approaches introduce a new risk. Prebunking requires identifying and labeling potential disinformation before it spreads. Doing so influences how audiences interpret information before events fully unfold. If institutions preemptively frame certain narratives as manipulation, they shape perception in advance. The same mechanisms used to defend against cognitive warfare can therefore influence interpretation in ways that resemble narrative control.  

This concern becomes more pronounced with targeted defensive models. Small language models designed for specific audiences allow highly tailored messaging. While intended to strengthen resilience, the same capability could be used to guide interpretation for particular populations. Messaging designed for narrowly defined groups begins to resemble targeted influence operations, even if deployed defensively. The distinction between protecting audiences and shaping their interpretation becomes less clear.  

This creates a broader risk. Countering cognitive warfare requires active intervention in the information environment. Large-scale intervention risks undermining trust if it appears that institutions are managing narratives. Efforts intended to defend against disinformation could be perceived as propaganda, particularly if they involve preemptive framing, selective messaging, or targeted influence.  

The challenge is balancing defense with transparency. Protecting the information ecosystem requires proactive measures, but those measures must avoid predetermining conclusions or suppressing legitimate debate. If counter-cognitive warfare evolves into narrative management, it risks producing the same erosion of trust that cognitive warfare seeks to create.

### <b id="Conclusion">Strategic Implications: Information Integrity as a National Security Requirement</b>  
Taken together, these discussions describe a shift in how conflict can be conducted. Cognitive warfare targets decision-making by creating uncertainty in the information environment. When trust in information declines, institutions hesitate, coordination slows, and responses degrade. As Ellen McCarthy argued, this makes the integrity of the information ecosystem comparable to foundational infrastructure. When that infrastructure is compromised, the effects propagate across government, industry, and society.  

Artificial intelligence increases pressure on this environment. David Pitts highlighted synthetic realities and deepfakes that shape perception at scale, while Shawn Chenoweth emphasized asymmetric competition in the information domain. AI lowers the cost of generating narratives, tailoring messaging, and flooding decision environments with competing claims. This shifts cognitive warfare from discrete campaigns to persistent pressure on the information ecosystem.  

Data poisoning further moves cognitive warfare toward infrastructure-level attacks. Instead of targeting audiences directly, adversaries can manipulate datasets, sources, and analytic pipelines. Corrupted inputs produce misleading outputs, introducing uncertainty before decision-makers even encounter the information. Protecting data integrity therefore becomes a core defensive requirement.  

Countering this environment begins with protecting information integrity. Preemptive measures such as prebunking, source validation, and targeted defensive tools can reduce the effectiveness of influence operations. However, these approaches also introduce a tension between defending the information ecosystem and shaping perception. Counter-cognitive warfare must preserve trust while actively defending against manipulation.  

The overall implication is that information integrity is becoming a national security requirement. Cognitive warfare targets trust, AI scales influence, and data poisoning threatens analytic foundations. Protecting the information ecosystem becomes necessary not only to counter disinformation, but to preserve the ability of institutions and societies to make decisions under uncertainty.
