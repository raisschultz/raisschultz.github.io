### <b id="Expands">AI Expands the Viable Target Set Across Critical Infrastructure</b>  

Throughout the AI+ Intelligence Summit, multiple speakers emphasized that artificial intelligence is changing which critical infrastructure systems are realistically worth targeting. Historically, many infrastructure environments—particularly smaller municipal utilities, local operators, and niche industrial systems—were not attractive targets for sophisticated attackers. Developing tailored exploits for unique configurations required manual reconnaissance, reverse engineering, and domain-specific expertise, making these environments inefficient targets compared to widely deployed technologies.  
Peter Ranks addressed this directly during the panel “New Era of Cybersecurity,” describing what he characterized as a vastly expanded attack surface across critical infrastructure. Ranks explained that smaller infrastructure operators, such as local water municipalities, historically avoided sophisticated attacks because creating custom exploits for those environments was not economical. Attackers would need to invest significant time researching unique configurations for systems that did not provide sufficient strategic or financial return. AI changes this calculation by enabling rapid generation of targeted exploits tailored to specific environments. Ranks emphasized that systems that previously “never had to worry about attackers” are now viable targets because AI dramatically reduces the effort required to develop specialized attacks.  
Rob Joyce reinforced this shift in the same panel, noting that AI systems can now analyze networks continuously and identify vulnerabilities across complex environments. Infrastructure deployments often rely on legacy technology, customized configurations, and fragmented architectures. These characteristics previously discouraged attackers, but AI-assisted reconnaissance reduces this protection by automatically surfacing misconfigurations and weaknesses that would previously require manual investigation.  
Earlier discussion in “AI and the Future of Global Security” provided a concrete example of this capability. David Cohen described a highly capable AI model, Claude Mythos Preview, which can scan systems, identify vulnerabilities across websites, codebases, and infrastructure, and chain those vulnerabilities into viable exploit paths. Cohen emphasized that the system could analyze entire environments and identify weaknesses that would previously require a dedicated vulnerability research team. When applied to critical infrastructure, this type of automated analysis allows attackers to identify vulnerabilities in specialized deployments without requiring domain-specific expertise.  
Together, these discussions describe a shift in attacker economics. AI reduces the time and expertise required to develop custom exploits, making smaller and more specialized infrastructure systems viable targets. Environments that previously benefited from obscurity, fragmentation, or limited attacker interest become increasingly exposed as reconnaissance and exploit generation are automated.  

### <b id="Acceleration">AI Accelerates Vulnerability Discovery Faster Than Infrastructure Can Respond</b>  
While AI expands the range of viable infrastructure targets, several speakers emphasized that critical infrastructure environments are structurally constrained in their ability to respond. AI-driven systems can continuously scan networks and identify vulnerabilities, but remediation in infrastructure environments remains limited by operational, technical, and organizational realities.  
Rob Joyce highlighted this mismatch during “New Era of Cybersecurity,” emphasizing that industry will not successfully patch all software vulnerabilities, particularly across industrial control systems and legacy infrastructure. Many infrastructure environments require manual updates, vendor coordination, or operational downtime before changes can be deployed. As AI increases the rate at which vulnerabilities are discovered, defenders cannot realistically remediate at the same pace. This creates a structural asymmetry where weaknesses can be identified faster than they can be addressed.  
Peter Ranks reinforced this constraint by emphasizing the challenge of deploying fixes across fragmented infrastructure environments. Tools and defenses are not standardized across industries, and infrastructure is often composed of heterogeneous systems operated by different organizations. Even when vulnerabilities are known, deploying patches across this distributed environment can be slow or infeasible. Infrastructure systems also prioritize uptime and operational continuity, making rapid patching difficult. Updates may require scheduled maintenance windows, safety validation, or coordination with vendors, further slowing defensive response.  
The result is a widening gap between AI-driven discovery and infrastructure remediation. Vulnerabilities can be identified continuously, while infrastructure operators remain bound by operational constraints. As discovery accelerates, weaknesses accumulate across infrastructure environments, increasing exposure particularly in sectors relying on legacy systems and distributed deployments.  

### <b id="Exposure">Uneven Security Across Critical Infrastructure Creates Systemic Exposure</b>  
Speakers also emphasized that critical infrastructure is not defended uniformly, and AI-driven targeting amplifies this disparity. Some infrastructure sectors maintain mature cybersecurity programs, while others operate with limited resources, legacy systems, and minimal defensive investment. When AI lowers the cost of vulnerability discovery and exploit development, attackers can selectively target weaker infrastructure environments rather than attempting to penetrate highly defended systems.  
Peter Ranks highlighted this imbalance during “New Era of Cybersecurity,” noting that cybersecurity investment across infrastructure is often driven by financial incentives rather than systemic risk. Sectors such as banking invest heavily in cybersecurity to protect financial assets and maintain trust, while smaller infrastructure operators—such as water management systems—historically have not faced the same pressure. As a result, these organizations often operate with weaker defenses, outdated technology, and slower patching cycles. AI reduces the effort required to identify these weaknesses, making less mature infrastructure increasingly attractive targets.  
Rob Joyce reinforced this dynamic by emphasizing that defenders must secure entire environments, while attackers only need to identify a single viable weakness. AI-driven reconnaissance allows attackers to scan multiple infrastructure environments and prioritize those with the lowest defensive posture. Rather than focusing on highly defended organizations, attackers can target smaller infrastructure systems with limited monitoring, fragmented security controls, or legacy deployments.  
Critical infrastructure is also highly distributed and interconnected. Weaknesses in smaller or less mature systems can create leverage points with broader operational impact. A compromise of a single infrastructure operator may affect dependent services, regional operations, or public trust. As AI lowers the cost of identifying and exploiting these weaker environments, risk shifts toward distributed infrastructure sectors where security maturity varies and defensive capabilities are inconsistent.    

### <b id="Battlefield">Critical Infrastructure Is Becoming a Primary Battlefield in AI-Enabled Conflict</b>  
Beyond expanding exposure, several speakers emphasized that critical infrastructure is increasingly being treated as a primary target in modern conflict. Cyber operations against infrastructure are no longer limited to espionage or disruption. Instead, infrastructure systems are being viewed as leverage points capable of producing strategic effects, including economic disruption, operational paralysis, and erosion of public trust. As AI lowers the cost of identifying and exploiting infrastructure vulnerabilities, more actors gain the ability to target these systems as part of conflict.  
Peter Ranks highlighted this shift during “New Era of Cybersecurity,” noting that critical infrastructure such as data centers, cloud providers, and energy systems now function as elements of national power. These systems enable economic activity, communications, and operational continuity, making them attractive targets in cyber-enabled conflict. Disrupting infrastructure can produce effects comparable to traditional military operations without requiring kinetic force. Ranks also referenced a cyberattack that wiped data from a bank used by both civilians and military elements, emphasizing that the impact extended beyond technical disruption. The attack undermined trust between the population and governing institutions, demonstrating how infrastructure attacks can generate psychological and political effects in addition to operational ones.  
Rob Joyce reinforced the idea that cyber operations targeting infrastructure now function as a form of hard power. Cyber capabilities already allow actors to project influence without conventional military engagement, and AI lowers the barrier for conducting these operations by automating vulnerability discovery and exploit development. As these capabilities become more accessible, infrastructure systems become viable targets for both state and non-state actors. This expands the battlefield beyond traditional military assets to include civilian and commercial infrastructure.  
Infrastructure targeting also scales with AI-enabled automation. When vulnerability discovery and exploit generation can be conducted across multiple environments simultaneously, attackers are no longer limited to isolated targets. Instead, distributed infrastructure systems can be evaluated and targeted in parallel. This increases the likelihood of successful compromise and raises the potential for cascading disruption across interconnected infrastructure sectors.   
Together, these developments describe a shift in the character of conflict. Critical infrastructure is no longer only a defensive concern or secondary target. It is increasingly treated as a primary battlefield where cyber operations are used to generate strategic effects. Artificial intelligence expands this battlefield by lowering the cost of targeting infrastructure, increasing the number of viable targets, and enabling more actors to conduct infrastructure-focused operations.  

### <b id="Conclusion">Conclusion</b>  
The discussions across the summit describe a shift in both cyber capability and the role of critical infrastructure in conflict. Artificial intelligence lowers the cost of vulnerability discovery and exploit development, making smaller and more specialized infrastructure systems viable targets. At the same time, operational constraints and uneven security maturity limit remediation across distributed infrastructure environments.  
Speakers emphasized that critical infrastructure is increasingly treated as a leverage point in modern conflict, where disruption can produce operational, economic, and psychological effects. As AI lowers the barrier for targeting infrastructure, more actors gain the ability to conduct operations that produce strategic impact.  
Taken together, these developments indicate that critical infrastructure is becoming a central battleground in modern warfare. AI expands the range of viable targets, lowers the barrier for infrastructure-focused operations, and increases the scale at which disruption can occur. As these capabilities spread, cyber operations against critical infrastructure become an increasingly prominent tool for generating strategic effects without kinetic force.
