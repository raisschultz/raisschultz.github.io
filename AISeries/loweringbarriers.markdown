### <b id="Automates">AI Automates Offensive Cyber Capabilities</b>   
  One of the most consistent themes across the summit discussions was that artificial intelligence is now automating the most technically demanding phases of offensive cyber operations. Tasks that previously required specialized expertise like vulnerability discovery, reconnaissance, exploit development, and attack path identification are increasingly being performed by AI systems successfully. This shift reduces the technical skill and resources needed to conduct meaningful cyber operations and compresses the time needed to move from discovery to exploitation.   
  
  David Cohen, former Deputy Director of the CIA, described a particularly clear example of this transition during the panel “AI and the Future of Global Security”. Cohen discussed the recent announcement of a new, highly capable AI model called Claude Mythos Preview, which is able to scan systems, identify vulnerabilities across websites, codebases, and infrastructure, and then chain those vulnerabilities together to generate viable exploits. He emphasized that the system could analyze an entire environment and identify weaknesses that would previously require a dedicated vulnerability research team. This capability automates one of the most technically demanding stages of cyber offense. Vulnerability discovery and exploit development historically required reverse engineering, manual testing, and deep domain expertise. AI systems performing these functions reduce the expertise and resource threshold required to identify and exploit weaknesses.   
  
  Rob Joyce, former NSA cybersecurity director and founder of Joyce Cyber, described a similar shift during the panel “New Era of Cybersecurity”. Joyce noted that cyber defenders have historically relied on understanding their own systems better than even their manufacturers, and by extension, attackers. He argued that AI has now undermined this advantage by allowing automated analysis of networks at scale. AI systems can continuously examine networks, identify misconfigurations, and surface vulnerabilities around the clock. This level of reconnaissance previously required skilled analysts and significant time investment. Automating reconnaissance reduces one of the major bottlenecks in offensive cyber operations and allows attackers to identify targets faster and more comprehensively.   
  
  Peter Ranks, Chief Strategy Officer at GRVTY, extended this argument by emphasizing that AI reduces the need for specialized talent. During the panel “New Era of Cybersecurity”, Ranks stated that work previously requiring expert practitioners can now be performed by individuals using AI tools. He specifically pointed to vulnerability discovery and exploit generation as areas where AI significantly lowers the barrier to entry. Instead of requiring deep technical knowledge, attackers can leverage AI systems to identify weaknesses and generate attack paths. This compresses the expertise required across multiple stages of cyber operations. 
  
  Ranks also highlighted that AI systems can generate custom exploits tailored to specific environments. This capability further reduces the need for manual exploit development. Historically, exploit creation required significant time, testing, and domain knowledge. AI-assisted exploit generation automates this process and allows attackers to move from vulnerability discovery to exploitation much more quickly. This shortens the attack lifecycle and increases the number of viable attack opportunities.   
  
  Sasha Baker, Head of National Security at OpenAI, provided additional context during the panel “AI and the Future of Global Security”. Baker described the need for trusted access programs restricting advanced cyber-capable models. These models are capable of scanning systems, identifying vulnerabilities, and assisting in cyber operations. The decision to restrict access reflects concern that such tools could enable malicious actors if broadly available. This reinforces the idea that AI systems are capable of automating meaningful portions of offensive cyber workflows.   
  
  Together, these discussions describe a shift from human-driven cyber operations to AI-assisted workflows. Reconnaissance, vulnerability discovery, and exploit generation are no longer purely manual processes requiring deep human expertise. AI systems can perform these tasks at scale and with reduced technical knowledge. This does not eliminate the need for human decision-making, but it lowers the technical barrier required to conduct offensive cyber operations. The result is a compression of expertise, where fewer specialized skills are required to move from identifying a target to developing a viable exploit.   

### <b id="Expands">AI Expands the Attack Surface and Viable Targets</b>  
  AI-driven automation does more than reduce the expertise required for cyber operations. It also expands the range of systems that can realistically be targeted. Multiple speakers emphasized that AI changes the economics of cyber offense by making previously impractical targets viable. Systems that were historically protected by obscurity, scale, or lack of attacker interest are now increasingly exposed.  
  
  Peter Ranks addressed this directly during the panel “New Era of Cybersecurity”. Ranks explained that many smaller infrastructure operators, such as local utilities, municipal systems, and niche industrial environments, were historically not attractive targets. Developing tailored exploits for these systems required significant time and specialized expertise, which made the return on investment too low for attackers. AI changes this calculation by enabling rapid generation of custom exploits for specific environments. Attackers can now target smaller systems because exploit development can be automated and scaled. This expands the viable target set and increases systemic exposure across infrastructure sectors.  
  
  David Cohen described a similar shift during “AI and the Future of Global Security”. In discussing AI systems capable of scanning entire environments and chaining vulnerabilities, Cohen noted that these capabilities enable targeting of niche systems that previously required dedicated expertise. When vulnerability discovery and exploit generation are automated, attackers are no longer constrained to widely deployed technologies. AI enables the identification and exploitation of unique configurations, custom software, and less common infrastructure. This broadens the attack surface beyond high-value, widely used systems.   
  
  Rob Joyce reinforced this concern by emphasizing the difficulty of patching vulnerabilities at the scale AI can discover them. During “New Era of Cybersecurity”, Joyce noted the challenge that industrial systems, legacy infrastructure, and distributed environments often cannot be patched quickly or uniformly. AI-driven vulnerability discovery increases the rate at which weaknesses are identified, while defenders remain constrained by operational realities. This creates a widening gap between discovery and remediation, particularly in critical infrastructure environments.   
  
  AI therefore does not only make attacks easier; it makes more systems worth attacking. As the cost of targeting decreases, the number of potential targets increases, particularly across distributed infrastructure sectors where security maturity varies widely. Systems that previously existed outside the focus of sophisticated attackers become viable targets.   

### <b id="Democratization">The Democratization of Cyber Capability</b>   
  As AI lowers the expertise required to conduct cyber operations and expands the number of viable targets, it also changes who can realistically carry out these attacks. Multiple speakers emphasized that AI is democratizing cyber capability by reducing the expertise, resources, and team size required to conduct meaningful operations. Capabilities that were previously limited to well-resourced organizations or highly skilled practitioners are becoming accessible to a broader range of actors.   
  
  Major General Robert Kinney addressed this shift during “Digital Innovation and Modernization at DIA”. Kinney emphasized that AI lowers the barrier of entry for threat actors and accelerates adversary capability development. As AI tools automate technical workflows such as vulnerability discovery and exploit generation, smaller actors gain access to capabilities that previously required dedicated teams. This compresses the time and expertise required to move from intent to execution and expands the range of actors capable of conducting cyber operations.   
  
  Peter Ranks reinforced this argument during “New Era of Cybersecurity”, stating that work previously requiring specialized practitioners can now be performed by individuals using AI tools. By automating vulnerability discovery, reconnaissance, and exploit generation, AI reduces reliance on deep technical expertise. Instead of requiring dedicated vulnerability researchers, attackers can leverage AI systems to identify weaknesses and generate attack paths. This lowers the skill threshold required to conduct sophisticated attacks and increases the number of actors capable of meaningful cyber activity.   
  
  Rob Joyce described a similar shift in capability distribution, noting that AI allows attackers to analyze systems at scale and identify vulnerabilities more efficiently than before. When these capabilities become widely accessible, smaller actors gain analytical power that previously required institutional resources. Sasha Baker provided additional context by discussing the need for trusted access programs restricting advanced cyber-capable models. These restrictions reflect concern that widely available models could enable malicious actors, underscoring the degree to which AI lowers the barrier to entry for offensive cyber operations.  
  
  Together, these discussions describe a transition from skill-constrained cyber capability to access-constrained capability. Previously, conducting sophisticated cyber operations required specialized knowledge and significant technical expertise. AI reduces these requirements by automating key technical processes. As a result, the limiting factor becomes access to capable AI systems rather than deep domain expertise, increasing the number of actors able to conduct meaningful cyber operations and contributing to a more distributed threat landscape.   

### <b id="Advantages">Offensive Advantages and Defensive Constraints</b>      
  While artificial intelligence lowers the barriers to conducting cyber operations and expands the number of capable actors, several speakers emphasized that these developments disproportionately benefit attackers. AI accelerates vulnerability discovery and exploit generation, but defenders remain constrained by patching timelines, operational limitations, and the complexity of real-world systems. This creates an asymmetry in which offense scales more easily than defense.   
  
  Rob Joyce addressed this imbalance during the panel “New Era of Cybersecurity”, emphasizing that defenders must secure entire environments, while attackers only need to identify a single viable vulnerability. AI amplifies this dynamic by increasing the speed and scale of vulnerability discovery. Automated analysis allows attackers to surface weaknesses continuously, whereas defenders must identify, prioritize, test, and deploy patches across complex environments. In industrial and legacy systems, this process can take significant time or may not be possible without disrupting operations, widening the gap between vulnerability identification and remediation.   
  
  Peter Ranks reinforced this asymmetry by highlighting the expansion of viable targets. As AI lowers the cost of exploit development, attackers can target more systems simultaneously, while defenders must maintain security across all systems regardless of whether they are actively targeted. This creates a structural disadvantage, where defensive resources are spread more thinly while attackers selectively focus on weaknesses identified by AI-driven analysis.   
  
  Joyce also emphasized that many infrastructure environments cannot be patched uniformly. Industrial control systems, legacy deployments, and distributed infrastructure often require manual updates, operational downtime, or vendor coordination. AI-driven vulnerability discovery operates at machine speed, but patching and remediation remain bound by operational realities. This mismatch further benefits attackers and increases pressure on defensive organizations attempting to keep pace with automated discovery.   
  
  The result is a structural shift favoring offense. AI not only lowers barriers for attackers but increases pressure on defenders, who remain constrained by operational complexity and the need to secure entire environments while attackers benefit from automation, speed, and selectivity.   

### <b id="Constraints">Constraints on AI-Enabled Cyber Operations</b>      
  Despite the lowering of technical barriers and expansion of offensive capability, speakers consistently emphasized that AI does not eliminate the need for human expertise. AI automates portions of the cyber attack lifecycle, but meaningful operations still require judgment, prioritization, and domain knowledge. These constraints limit the extent to which barriers are lowered and prevent AI-enabled cyber operations from becoming fully autonomous.   
  
  Rob Joyce emphasized during “New Era of Cybersecurity” that AI primarily identifies vulnerabilities created by human misconfiguration rather than discovering entirely new classes of weaknesses. This means that fundamental cybersecurity practices still matter. Proper configuration, network segmentation, and patching discipline can reduce exposure even in an environment where AI accelerates vulnerability discovery. AI changes the speed and scale of analysis, but it does not fundamentally alter the underlying causes of most vulnerabilities.   
  
  Peter Ranks also highlighted the continued importance of human decision-making. While AI can identify vulnerabilities and generate potential exploit paths, attackers must still determine which targets are valuable and which attack paths are operationally feasible. Decisions regarding timing, persistence, and operational objectives remain dependent on human judgment. Several speakers also emphasized that operational deployment remains constrained by real-world requirements such as gaining access, maintaining persistence, and adapting to defensive responses. These steps cannot be fully automated and still require technical knowledge and coordination. 
  
  Another limitation discussed was the need for domain expertise when interpreting AI output. AI systems can surface vulnerabilities and generate suggested attack paths, but these outputs must be evaluated for accuracy and feasibility. Misinterpretation or overreliance on automated output can lead to ineffective or detectable operations. AI therefore functions as an augmentation tool rather than a replacement for expertise.  
  
  These constraints indicate that AI compresses expertise rather than eliminating it. The technical skill required to identify vulnerabilities and generate exploits decreases, but operational planning, targeting decisions, and execution still require knowledge and judgment. AI-enabled cyber operations therefore remain human-directed, even as portions of the technical workflow become automated.    

### <b id="Implications">Strategic Implications and Conclusions</b>      
  Taken together, the discussions across the summit describe a structural shift in cyber operations. Artificial intelligence is lowering the technical and operational barriers required to conduct meaningful cyber attacks by automating vulnerability discovery, reconnaissance, exploit generation, and attack path identification. Tasks that previously required specialized expertise and significant time investment can increasingly be performed by AI systems, reducing the skill and resource threshold required to move from discovery to exploitation.    
  
  As these barriers fall, the range of viable targets expands. AI-driven exploit generation makes it economically feasible to target niche infrastructure, smaller organizations, and unique system configurations that historically avoided sophisticated attacks. Systems that were previously protected by obscurity or lack of attacker interest become viable targets once reconnaissance and exploit development can be automated. This expands the attack surface, particularly across distributed infrastructure environments where security maturity varies widely.   
  
  At the same time, AI democratizes cyber capability. Automation reduces reliance on specialized practitioners and enables smaller actors to perform tasks that previously required dedicated teams. This shifts cyber capability from being primarily skill-constrained to increasingly access-constrained. As access to capable AI systems expands, more actors gain meaningful offensive capability. The result is a more distributed threat environment where sophisticated cyber operations are no longer limited to well-resourced organizations.    
  
  These developments also create an asymmetry favoring offense. AI accelerates vulnerability discovery and exploit development, while defenders remain constrained by patching timelines, operational limitations, and system complexity. Attackers only need to identify a single viable weakness, whereas defenders must secure entire environments. As AI increases the speed and scale of discovery, the gap between offensive capability and defensive response widens, particularly in legacy and critical infrastructure environments.    
  
  Despite these changes, AI does not eliminate the need for expertise. Human judgment remains necessary for prioritization, targeting, and operational execution. AI compresses expertise rather than removing it. Technical workflows become increasingly automated, but strategic decision-making and deployment remain human-directed. This limits full automation while still lowering the threshold required to conduct meaningful operations.   
  
  The combined effect of these trends is a shift in how cyber capability is generated and distributed. AI lowers technical barriers, expands the attack surface, democratizes capability, and amplifies offensive advantage. More actors can target more systems with less expertise and fewer resources. This represents a transition from a cyber environment constrained primarily by specialized talent to one increasingly constrained by access to AI-enabled tools.
